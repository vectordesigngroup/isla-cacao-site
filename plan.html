Cacao Action Plan: Venezuelan Fine-Flavor Beans → Daniel's Own Chocolate Brand

Prepared Oct 20, 2026 (America/New_York). Builds on coconut-cacao-export.md. Read that report first for the price, tariff, sanctions and logistics background.

Who does what - Daniel (Gainesville, FL): US entity, buyers, compliance, brand. - Dad (Margarita Island, retired ex-Deloitte audit partner): on-the-ground quality control, supplier vetting, books and controls.

Two phases - Phase 1 (now to ~6 months): bean import and sourcing. Prove one compliant, profitable lot. 0. Executive summary

  • Recommended starting model: a small importer of record with pre-sold beans (section 3).
  • Daniel's Florida LLC imports one 300–500 kg lot of fine-flavor beans that a US maker has already committed to buy, with part of the lot reserved for brand R&D.
  • A pure broker model is lower risk but does not secure supply for the brand.
  • Brand R&D can start right away using beans bought domestically from US importers already carrying Venezuelan origins, which needs no import paperwork.
  • Top 3 suppliers to contact first:
  • Casa Franceschi / Cacao San José C.A. (Carúpano, Paria): sea-shipment records show regular US deliveries.
  • Intercacao (Río Caribe and Sur del Lago processing centers, with a Miami office).
  • Roraima Cocoa Trading (Caracas): says it has FDA registration and an FSVP in place, and offers FOB, CIF or US-warehouse delivery.
  • Top 3 buyers to pitch:
  • Castronovo Chocolate (Stuart, FL): already makes Venezuelan Chuao and Patanemo bars.
  • Escazú Chocolates (Raleigh, NC): co-owner is Venezuelan; publicly said it has had trouble getting Venezuelan beans into the US.
  • Amano Chocolate (Utah): makes Ocumare and Cuyagua bars.
  • Pilot budget: about US$7.5k–$15k (est.) for a 300–500 kg lot including legal, testing and freight. See section 6.
  • Hard gate: no money is wired to Venezuela until a sanctions screen is clean, lab results pass, a US buyer's purchase order is signed and a sanctions attorney has signed off (section 5).

4B. Venezuela (exporter's obligations; Dad verifies them)

The Venezuelan exporter of record should be an established, registered company, not Daniel or Dad personally, unless the sanctions attorney approves otherwise.

  1. Exporter registrations: RIF; VUCE (single export window); BCV foreign-exchange registration and export declaration (AVEX). Also INSAI registrations (SIGESAI/RUNSAI) and a phytosanitary movement permit (INSAI).
  2. State cacao body: INSAI's list includes registration with the Corporación Socialista del Cacao Venezolano. A secondary post also mentions SUNAGRO and the anti-drug command (LinkedIn post, ⚠ secondary). The corporation is a government body, so a US person must not be the party dealing with it beyond what OFAC GL 35 permits (fees, permits, registrations, with USD-payment reporting) (GL 35). Another reason to use an established exporter.
  3. INSAI phytosanitary certificate: request inspection 48 h in advance with a request letter, sworn statement, lab or fumigation results, commercial invoice, fee receipt, booking or B/L and the DUA. The certificate is issued after inspection (INSAI).
  4. Customs (SENIAT): DUA through SIDUNEA. Simplified document rules since Sep 14, 2026 (Noticiero Digital).
  5. Foreign-exchange rule: the exporter keeps 80% and sells 20% to the BCV within 5 days, under Convenio Cambiario No. 1 of 2018 (AVEX). ⚠ Confirm it still applies.

4C. Quality grading and lab tests (Dad's core role)

  • Standard: Venezuela grades beans under COVENIN 50 using a cut test that checks fermentation and defects (moldy, slaty, insect-damaged, germinated, flat). F1 means better fermentation and fewer defects; F2 is the lower grade (Puro Chocolate, Gaceta 6.695 via FAOLEX). Roraima says it grades all lots to COVENIN 50 (Roraima). Target F1 for craft buyers.
  • Moisture: a secondary summary of COVENIN puts the maximum at 8%; many buyers want about 7% (⚠ confirm against the standard and the buyer's spec).
  • Dad's on-site checks: an independent cut test on 100+ beans per lot (est. method); a moisture meter; a smell check for smoky or moldy off-notes; matching bag counts and weights to the paperwork; photos and seal numbers.
  • US lab tests (pre-shipment sample, plus arrival sample if buyers want one):
  • Heavy metals (As, Cd, Pb, Hg): about $160–164 at Medallion Labs, or $75 each for Cd or Pb alone (Medallion). Other labs quote $190–252 (IAS, SimpleLab).
  • Also consider: ochratoxin A, pesticide residues and Salmonella (⚠ prices not gathered).
  • Why cadmium matters for the brand: California Prop 65 chocolate settlement levels. For products at or below 65% cacao, warnings are triggered above 0.100 ppm lead / 0.400 ppm cadmium; for 65–95% cacao, 0.150 / 0.450 ppm (Grimaldi Law summary, CA AG consent judgment). Test beans before buying a lot. Casa Franceschi's shipment records mention "Low Cadmium" lots (ImportYeti).

7B. Labeling and allergens (federal)

  • Standards of identity: cacao products are standardized in 21 CFR Part 163. For example, sweet chocolate needs at least 15% chocolate liquor, and milk chocolate needs at least 10% chocolate liquor, 3.39% milkfat and 12% milk solids (eCFR 163). Names like "dark chocolate" and "milk chocolate" must comply.
  • Allergens: FDA's nine major allergens (milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame, effective Jan 1, 2023) (FDA). Note any shared-equipment risk honestly, especially for milk and nuts in co-man facilities.
  • Nutrition Facts: a small-business exemption exists (fewer than 100 FTE and fewer than 100,000 units, with an annual FDA notice; lost if nutrient claims are made) (FDA guide). ⚠ Check whether Daniel qualifies as importer and brand owner, and note that Amazon and most retailers expect Nutrition Facts anyway.
  • Origin and quality claims: say "Venezuela, Río Caribe" (for example) only with lot traceability. Do not use "Chuao" without an Empresa Campesina de Chuao certificate.
  • California Prop 65: test finished bars against the settlement levels in section 4C before selling into California.

7C. Packaging

  • Inner wrap that blocks moisture and odor (foil or compostable film), with an outer sleeve or box. Co-manufacturers such as Bitzel's and Cocoa Pack offer wrapping in-house (see links).
  • Heat: Bitzel's ships with insulated packaging and ice packs whenever the destination is 70°F or warmer (Bitzel's). Plan summer direct-to-consumer shipping costs accordingly (est. a major margin hit in Florida summers).

7E. Custom molds from Daniel's 3D printers (food-safety caveats)

  • Do not put chocolate in raw 3D-printed molds (PLA, PETG or resin). Layer lines trap residue and bacteria, filament additives are often not food-grade, brass nozzles can contain lead, and PLA cannot take sanitizing heat (Meshcast).
  • Safe routes:
  • Print a master, then cast food-contact silicone. Use a platinum-cure silicone with a real food-contact statement; Smooth-On's bulletin cites Smooth-Sil 940/950/960, Sorta Clear and Equinox as complying with 21 CFR 177.2600 for repeated use (PrintForge summary, Meshcast silicone guide). Only the silicone touches chocolate.
  • Vacuum-form food-safe plastic (for example HDPE) over a printed master (Formlabs). Polycarbonate production molds (est.) are what professional shops typically use. A co-manufacturer can also build molds from Daniel's design: Bitzel's $525 in-house mold service is one example.
  • Brand idea (est.): a signature bar shape and relief (for example a map of the Paria peninsula or a cacao-pod pattern) designed in CAD, prototyped on his printers, then turned into silicone or polycarbonate molds.
